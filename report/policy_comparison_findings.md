# What the documented platforms and campaigns show

This is a source-backed, case-comparative analysis—not a claim that every candidate in the national registry has a recovered complete platform. It studies attributed positions and candidate-authored campaign framing, not private beliefs or voter preferences.

## The Arizona2018 campaign archives show a real insurer-role disagreement alongside ACA overlap

Westbrook's candidate-authored article explicitly proposes tax-financed single payer and eliminating private health insurers, while Tipirneni's plan calls for Medicare buy-in and competition with private insurers. Their early platforms also share support for preserving existing ACA coverage. Westbrook specifies free public-college tuition and paid parental leave; Tipirneni emphasizes apprenticeships and job protection during family or medical emergencies. The latter text does not specify paid status, so silence is not coded as opposition.

**Evidence:** `az18-westbrook-tipirneni-health | az18-westbrook-tipirneni-private-insurance | az18-westbrook-tipirneni-aca | az18-westbrook-tipirneni-education | az18-westbrook-tipirneni-leave`

**Limit:** The sources are early and pre-primary congressional-campaign versions, not a complete census of the campaign. Westbrook's article says Medicaid in one line and Medicare for All in a heading; the inconsistency is preserved rather than silently repaired. A later state-house platform reached through a redirect was excluded.

## A blanket data-center objection differs from conditional approval, while transit commitments overlap

In the District39 Maryland questionnaire, Gabriel Acevero explicitly opposes additional data centers. George Lluberes, Lesley Lopez, Greg Wims and Robert Wu instead specify different conditions on growth, costs, labor, resource use or siting. All five nevertheless support expanding public transportation. Acevero and Lopez also both affirm ending287(g) cooperation, while the candidates emphasize different additional services and protections for immigrant communities.

**Evidence:** `md26-acevero-lluberes-data | md26-acevero-lopez-data | md26-acevero-wims-data | md26-acevero-wu-data | md26-acevero-lluberes-transit | md26-acevero-lopez-transit | md26-acevero-wims-transit | md26-acevero-wu-transit | md26-acevero-lopez-immigration`

**Limit:** The exact pre-primary version of these republished Vote411 answers is unverified, so comparisons remain timing-qualified and the full answers do not enter the dated model corpus. Amar Mukunda is not covered by the publisher guide; no nonresponse or withdrawal is inferred.

## Medicare-for-All wording does not establish an identical insurance design

Sanders' retained2020 platform proposes national single-payer coverage free at point of service. Yang's dedicated page uses the Medicare-for-All title but qualifies his support as its spirit, rejects rapid private-insurance elimination and emphasizes cost and delivery reform. Williamson's dedicated healthcare page explicitly offers Medicare through ACA exchanges and retains private choice, despite more general Medicare-for-all language on her economy page. Gleib combines Medicare expansion for uninsured people with ACA repair, private choice and ending job-tied coverage.

**Evidence:** `hpf20-sanders-yang-health | hpf20-sanders-williamson-health | hpf20-sanders-gleib-health`

**Limit:** These are specific dated campaign-source snapshots, not proof that each candidate's position was unchanged throughout the campaign. Topic or phrase matching is not a substitute for reading the financing and transition qualifications.

## Debt forgiveness and income security differ in explicit scope and mechanism

Sanders and Williamson both explicitly propose canceling all existing college/student debt, while Gleib proposes forgiving half and easing repayment of the remainder. Sanders specifies an employment guarantee at a living wage; Yang and Williamson specify monthly cash basic income. Williamson's economy page says all American adults in one place and ages18-65 in an embedded response, so the dataset retains that eligibility ambiguity rather than silently selecting one rule.

**Evidence:** `hpf20-sanders-williamson-debt | hpf20-sanders-gleib-debt | hpf20-sanders-yang-income | hpf20-sanders-williamson-income`

**Limit:** The income mechanisms may coexist; these passages do not establish blanket opposition to the other approach. Projected effects and candidate fiscal estimates are not independently verified. Shared national sources are not multiplied into independent state-level observations.

## Common Wisconsin governor prompts reveal different tools and priorities, alongside shared commitments

In PBS Wisconsin's early2026 interviews, Francesca Hong and Kelda Roys both support zoning changes to increase housing supply. Hong additionally names land trusts and accessory dwellings, while Mandela Barnes foregrounds corporate property concentration and Sara Rodriguez describes coordinating rules and funding. On school vouchers, Roys explicitly calls for responsible closure; Hong sharply criticizes the program but does not specify closure in this selected answer. All six tracked Democrats describe bipartisan work positively. Their first-bill answers differ more clearly: Hong begins with gun safety and children-focused measures, Barnes prioritizes BadgerCare expansion, and other candidates emphasize affordability through healthcare, housing, schools or public-system buy-in.

**Evidence:** `wi26-hong-roys-housing | wi26-hong-barnes-housing | wi26-hong-rodriguez-housing | wi26-hong-roys-vouchers | wi26-hong-barnes-priorities | wi26-hong-rodriguez-priorities | wi26-hong-brennan-priorities | wi26-hong-crowley-priorities | wi26-hong-roys-priorities | wi26-hong-barnes-bipartisan | wi26-hong-rodriguez-bipartisan | wi26-hong-brennan-bipartisan | wi26-hong-crowley-bipartisan | wi26-hong-roys-bipartisan`

**Limit:** These are six common topic responses per tracked candidate from an edited broadcast series, not complete final platforms. They predate later withdrawals and do not prove continued activity at the primary. Other people interviewed by PBS are not silently added to the tracked ballot field, and the candidates' claimed records are not independently verified.

## The 2017 Bay Ridge interviews cut across a simple socialist-versus-moderate divide

Khader El-Yateem and Nancy Tong both oppose supervised injection facilities in their2017 interviews and support more affordable housing for residents displaced by unsafe conversions. El-Yateem makes preserving parking a condition of bike-share support, while Justin Brannan emphasizes waterfront recreation and Kevin Carroll emphasizes public participation in siting. All three opponents' answers supply specific local-service proposals. Brannan also describes municipal constituent work as activism, while Carroll explicitly presents himself as a moderate. These actual statements are more informative than assigning positions from endorsement labels.

**Evidence:** `ny17-elyateem-tong-injection-sites | ny17-elyateem-tong-housing | ny17-elyateem-brannan-bikes | ny17-elyateem-carroll-bikes | ny17-elyateem-brannan-sanitation | ny17-elyateem-carroll-sanitation | ny17-elyateem-tong-sanitation | ny17-elyateem-brannan-frame | ny17-elyateem-carroll-frame`

**Limit:** Four of the five Democratic candidates have reviewed published interview answers, not complete platforms; Vincent Chirico remains missing. Brannan's injection-site stance is only a reporter paraphrase in this source, so it is not used to invent a literal quoted disagreement with El-Yateem. The historical statements are not medical advice or present-day positions.

## Single-payer produces real disagreements, but not a uniform endorsement-group split

Brandon West supports the New York Health Act while Briget Rein explicitly rejects single-payer and favors union-negotiated coverage. Illapa Sairitupac prioritizes single-payer while Denny Salas favors a public option and rejects single-payer. But Sairitupac and Grace Lee share support, as do Melat Kiros and Diana DeGette. In the timing-qualified Illinois 2020 guide, Anthony Clark, Danny Davis and Kina Collins all endorse Medicare for All, while Kristine Schanbacher proposes an ACA public option. The candidate-dated Massachusetts 2020 questionnaires, whose first public-release timing remains qualified, show an important jurisdictional distinction: Erika Uyterhoeven supports Massachusetts single payer; Catia Sharp rejects a state-only plan while supporting federal single payer. In a different Massachusetts race, both Anna Callahan and Christine Barber support state single payer, although Barber also proposes a public-option step toward it.

**Evidence:** `cmp-joldc-west-rein-single-payer | cmp-joldc-37-38-healthcare | cmp-joldc-37-40-healthcare | co01-healthcare-alignment-2026 | il20-clark-davis-health | il20-clark-collins-health | il20-clark-schanbacher-health | ny16-bowman-engel-health | ny16-bowman-ravelo-health | ny22-alexis-mays-health | ma20-uyterhoeven-sharp-state-payer | ma20-callahan-barber-state-payer | ma20-callahan-barber-health-route`

**Limit:** These examples establish the presence of both disagreement and overlap, not their prevalence across the full national candidate population.

## Housing contrasts often concern complementary tools rather than opposing goals

Izola Shaw emphasizes public housing-production financing while Allison Eriksen emphasizes zoning reform. Aparna Raj combines housing supply and tenant protection while Miguel Deramo emphasizes that access requires more homes. Mamdani and Ramos both support a rent freeze. In DC Ward4, Janeese Lewis George combines tenant protections with deeper affordability and public investment; Brandon Todd emphasizes housing delivery and funding already secured; Marlena Edwards emphasizes replicating a particular affordable-development model. Across these examples, different tools often coexist rather than constituting opposing platforms.

**Evidence:** `cmp-hist-a-10-007 | cmp-hb06-raj-deramo-housing | cmp-joldc-24-26-housing | cmp-joldc-24-28-housing | dc20-george-todd-housing | dc20-george-edwards-housing`

**Limit:** The retained answers are not exhaustive platform inventories. A tool omitted from one answer remains unknown, not rejected.

## Implementation strategy and campaign emphasis can differ even when goals overlap

Derrick Crowe emphasizes registering and mobilizing new Democratic voters, whereas Joseph Kopser emphasizes Republican crossover support. Kaniela Ing emphasizes starting with a bold progressive agenda while Ed Case emphasizes finding cross-party agreement. In other examples, Kiros and DeGette share a healthcare goal but describe different routes to passage; Mamdani explicitly centers affordability while Lander names ending street homelessness as his first campaign commitment.

**Evidence:** `co01-healthcare-passage-strategy-2026 | parent-mamdani-lander-explicit-priorities | tx18-crowe-kopser-coalition | hi18-ing-case-strategy | hi18-ing-fukumoto-strategy | il20-clark-davis-strategy | il20-clark-schanbacher-strategy`

**Limit:** This describes candidates' stated strategies and self-defined priorities, not measured spending, message frequency, or causal electoral effects.

## Support for the same program can carry materially different conditions

In the 2020 South Bronx primary, Samelys Lopez calls for single payer without patient charges and includes undocumented residents. Ritchie Torres supports a version of single payer with explicit labor-buy-in, Bronx-funding and Bronx-job conditions. They nevertheless both support healthcare for undocumented residents, supervised consumption sites and sex-work decriminalization. Preserving those conditions gives a more faithful comparison than labeling Torres simply anti-single-payer.

**Evidence:** `ny15-lopez-torres-single-payer | ny15-lopez-torres-immigrant-health | ny15-lopez-torres-consumption | ny15-lopez-torres-sex-work`

**Limit:** Two candidates' documented positions do not cover the entire twelve-candidate primary, and support for one measure does not imply identical broader platforms.

## Campaigns also differ over how representatives should exercise power

In Missouri's 2022 primary, Cori Bush presents direct action for an eviction-moratorium extension as part of her congressional role; Steve Roberts contrasts street activism with work inside the Capitol while acknowledging shared concerns. In New York's 2020 District16 primary, Jamaal Bowman emphasizes inside-outside movement power while Eliot Engel emphasizes Democratic leadership relationships, even though both explicitly support Medicare for All and a Green New Deal. The timing-qualified Illinois 2020 guide shows another contrast: Anthony Clark emphasizes collective working-class power, Danny Davis emphasizes his progressive record, and Kristine Schanbacher emphasizes transferring her human-rights legal work to Congress.

**Evidence:** `mo22-bush-roberts-activism | ny16-bowman-engel-strategy | ny16-bowman-engel-health | ny16-bowman-engel-climate | il20-clark-davis-strategy | il20-clark-schanbacher-strategy`

**Limit:** These are candidates' self-presentations, not independent measures of legislative productivity, movement strength, or electoral effectiveness.

## Foreign-policy differences require specifying the actual condition or instrument

The Ohio special-primary questionnaires combine shared support for a two-state solution with differing aid restrictions, negotiating conditions, and diplomatic sequences. General alliance support is not evidence of agreement on every weapons transfer or aid use.

**Evidence:** `oh21-turner-brown-two-state | oh21-turner-johnson-two-state | oh21-turner-brown-aid | oh21-turner-brown-iran`

**Limit:** These are candidates' attributed 2021 positions. The analysis neither adjudicates their factual claims nor substitutes them for present-day positions.

## Public-safety comparisons need the specific intervention, not a blanket label

In the 2020 DC Ward4 questionnaires, Lewis George prioritizes Cure the Streets and Todd also supports violence-interruption funding; Edwards emphasizes community policing and police-school relationships. In the 2022 NY Senate21 questionnaires, David Alexis supports Fair and Timely Parole while Kaegan Mays-Williams requests amendments, yet both support the New York Health Act, Good Cause Eviction and full sex-work decriminalization. These combinations do not fit a single binary ideological label.

**Evidence:** `dc20-george-todd-safety | dc20-george-edwards-safety | ny22-alexis-mays-parole | ny22-alexis-mays-health | ny22-alexis-mays-good-cause | ny22-alexis-mays-sex-work`

**Limit:** Support for one public-health or policing intervention is not proof of rejecting every other measure. Former occupation and endorsement category are not substitutes for the actual answer.

## Conditions and uncertainty cannot be flattened into opposition

In the Saval-Farnese 2020 questionnaires, Saval calls for a fracking ban; Farnese supports a ban at least while permitting is reviewed. Both support supervised consumption sites, but Farnese adds a host-community consent condition. Both favor community land trusts and an AB5-style worker-classification law. The evidence distinguishes duration, conditions and implementation rather than inventing blanket ideological opposition.

**Evidence:** `pa20-saval-farnese-fracking | pa20-saval-farnese-consumption | pa20-saval-farnese-land-trusts | pa20-saval-farnese-ab5`

**Limit:** The publisher revised Saval's page in 2024, so these comparisons retain a version-timing qualification even though the original publication is dated 2020.

## Complete fields reveal shared commitments to public ownership and development safeguards

In Pittsburgh's 2023 District 7 questionnaire, Deb Gross and Jordan Botta both support public housing and inclusionary zoning while differing over additional tools and police staffing. In Allegheny County District 11, Dennis McDermott and Paul Klein both oppose privatizing Shuman and favor more council resources. All five Wisconsin Assembly District 76 candidates support a hyperscale data-center moratorium, although their proposed duration and safeguards differ. Bennett and Martinez-Rutherford also both support restoring rent-regulation authority.

**Evidence:** `pa23-gross-botta-public-housing | pa23-gross-botta-inclusionary | pa23-mcdermott-klein-shuman | pa23-mcdermott-klein-resources | wi26-bennett-benami-data-centers | wi26-bennett-castaneda-data-centers | wi26-bennett-martinez-data-centers | wi26-bennett-sullivan-data-centers | wi26-bennett-martinez-rent-control`

**Limit:** These full questionnaire fields improve within-race comparison, not national representativeness. Near-duplicate Bennett/Sullivan public-bank responses are separately flagged and do not support a reviewed agreement.

## The same pair of candidates can agree on one ballot proposal and disagree on another

In their September 2016 questionnaire, Mike Connolly supports recreational-cannabis legalization while Tim Toomey opposes that ballot question at the time, despite supporting prior decriminalization and medical measures. Both oppose the current charter-cap ballot proposal, even while disputing their records. Toomey also promises to implement legalization if voters approve it. Keeping the exact proposal and time frame avoids reducing a candidate to an across-the-board label.

**Evidence:** `ma16-connolly-toomey-cannabis | ma16-connolly-toomey-charters | ma16-connolly-toomey-treatment`

**Limit:** These are the candidates' stated 2016 positions, not their present-day views or independent assessments of their records.

## Exact questionnaire items expose disagreements that broad ideological labels miss

Cass Green and Sajda Blackwell both support state single-payer healthcare covering undocumented residents and a Pennsylvania Green New Deal. Their 2024 questionnaire marks differ on a moratorium on new charter schools and on ending the death penalty in all cases: Green supports both propositions and Blackwell marks no. Their longer answers also differ over infrastructure ownership, with Green opposing privatization and Blackwell considering private partnerships with community-input requirements.

**Evidence:** `pa24-cass-blackwell-health | pa24-cass-blackwell-climate | pa24-cass-blackwell-charters | pa24-cass-blackwell-death-penalty | pa24-cass-blackwell-infrastructure`

**Limit:** Unexplained checkboxes are reported only for their exact questions, not as a complete account of either candidate's philosophy. These two questionnaires do not cover incumbent Amen Brown's platform.

## Different levels of commitment are not automatically opposing policies

In the 2024 Assembly82 questionnaires, Jonathan Soto affirms a sex-work decriminalization bill while Michael Benedetto says he is open to learning more. Both separately support removing Vice Squads. In Assembly37, Claire Valdez and Johanna Carmona both support the named decriminalization bill and practical assistance for reproductive healthcare, while differing in housing and public-safety strategies. These distinctions require reading each answer in context rather than treating every less-specific answer as opposition.

**Evidence:** `ny24-soto-benedetto-sex-work | ny24-soto-benedetto-vice | ny24-valdez-carmona-sex-work | ny24-valdez-carmona-abortion | ny24-valdez-carmona-housing | ny24-valdez-carmona-safety`

**Limit:** These are selected source-backed comparisons within specific primaries. They do not establish that all other Democrats take the same positions or that these questionnaires cover every candidate in each field.

## Minnesota candidates combine shared labor and housing goals with different governing approaches

Sheigh Freeberg, Sandy Pappas and Zuki Ellis all support expanding workers' organizing or bargaining rights in their2022 Senate65 materials. Freeberg foregrounds community housing grants and school funding; Pappas emphasizes bonding, paid leave and pensions; Ellis combines public housing and tenant protection. Freeberg presents a people-before-profit frame while Pappas describes pragmatic progressive compromise. Ellis's nonresponse to one publisher is not treated as no platform because her separate Senate site was recovered.

**Evidence:** `mn22-freeberg-pappas-unions | mn22-freeberg-ellis-unions | mn22-freeberg-pappas-housing | mn22-freeberg-ellis-housing | mn22-freeberg-pappas-budget | mn22-freeberg-pappas-framing`

**Limit:** The sources have different formats. These statements do not measure message frequency, campaign spending, or legislative effectiveness, and Freeberg is not characterized as rejecting all compromise.

## A negative checkbox can conceal a conditional position that the explanation makes clear

In the 2023 Philadelphia District7 questionnaires, Andrés Celin marks no on overdose-prevention centers but says he can support them if accompanying neighborhood-safety measures are implemented at the same time. Quetcy Lozada rules out her district while supporting a center elsewhere under stated conditions. They disagree more directly on reducing school-police reliance and committing to a fossil-fuel ban in new buildings, while both support affordable housing production and the public-bank initiative named in the questionnaire.

**Evidence:** `pa23-celin-lozada-overdose | pa23-celin-lozada-school-police | pa23-celin-lozada-fossil-buildings | pa23-celin-lozada-housing | pa23-celin-lozada-public-bank`

**Limit:** Lozada's PDF has intrinsic2023 context but no verified pre-primary release version, so these pairs remain timing-qualified. Her support for an eventual fossil-fuel ban is preserved rather than mislabeled as categorical opposition to decarbonization.

## Candidates can disagree over what belongs in a local campaign

In the 2024 NY14 primary, Marty Dolan presents the Gaza war as peripheral to district concerns. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez explicitly argues that international policy and spending translate into local resource decisions. This is a documented disagreement in campaign focus, not an independently established finding about constituents' priorities. The 2020 NY14 relief debate likewise contrasts scrutiny of corporate-favoring program design with passing aid and pursuing further legislation, without reducing either candidate to a blanket pro- or anti-relief label.

**Evidence:** `ny24-aoc-dolan-local-global | ny20-aoc-caruso-relief`

**Limit:** The2020 reports discuss overlapping coronavirus measures, so the analysis does not invent a precisely identified common statutory vote. Campaign rhetoric is not measured public opinion or proof of policy effectiveness.

## Correct opponents and current statements matter more than a simple incumbent–challenger story

The official2020 Douglas County returns place Shannon Portillo against Karen Willey in District3, and Shannon Reid against Nancy Thellman and Sara Taliaferro in District2. In the correct fields, both Portillo and Willey discuss alternatives to jail expansion, while Reid and Taliaferro oppose the current expansion project. Thellman describes reconsidering the project during the pandemic rather than simply restating her earlier yes vote. Comparing an old vote with an opponent's current statement would miss that change.

**Evidence:** `ks20-portillo-willey-jail | ks20-reid-taliaferro-jail | ks20-reid-thellman-jail | ks20-reid-thellman-representation`

**Limit:** These are attributed primary-period positions and stated goals, not proof of identical commitments or a later vote audit. The earlier crossed district assignment and erroneous unopposed flag are corrected against the retained official results.

## Criminal-justice comparisons often show shared goals with different tools

In the2018 Clark County district-attorney race, Rob Langford and Steve Wolfson both describe release for defendants who present neither flight risk nor danger. Their bail-reform and capital-litigation arguments nevertheless differ. In Louisville's2020 District10 questionnaire, Ryan Fenwick and Pat Mulvihill both support civilian police review, while Fenwick foregrounds prevention and the spending mix and Mulvihill foregrounds warrants, auditing, recruitment and training. Neither example supports treating every non-endorsed Democrat as opposing reform.

**Evidence:** `f2-langford-wolfson-pretrial | f2-langford-wolfson-bail | f2-langford-wolfson-capital | ky20-fenwick-mulvihill-oversight | ky20-fenwick-mulvihill-safety`

**Limit:** The Louisville current edition has a known post-primary revision and remains timing-qualified. Langford is not coded as a categorical death-penalty abolitionist, and the review-board proposals do not grant identical powers.

## A shared named housing fund can coexist with different development priorities

Irma Corado and James Walkinshaw both support the Penny for Affordable Housing fund and greater housing diversity in Fairfax's2019 Braddock District questionnaire. Corado emphasizes affordability, displacement safeguards and commuter access; Walkinshaw specifies funding allocations and transportation projects. Their differing detail and emphasis do not establish a blanket housing-production disagreement.

**Evidence:** `f2-corado-walkinshaw-housing-fund | f2-corado-walkinshaw-diversity | f2-corado-walkinshaw-development | f2-corado-walkinshaw-transit`

**Limit:** This is a complete six-question source for the two candidates, not a complete inventory of everything either campaign said.

## An opponent's campaign attack is not a substitute for their own platform

The original2020 Oregon primary pamphlet preserves the candidates' submitted statements side by side. Mark Gamba says Kurt Schrader opposes universal healthcare, while Schrader's own statement says he has worked for universal healthcare and emphasizes the ACA and prescription costs. Both also describe climate action, using different named approaches. In the adjacent Fourth District, Doyle Canning names Medicare for All while Peter DeFazio states a universal-access goal. These broad goals do not prove identical financing plans, but the candidates' own statements prevent coding every attack as an opponent's position.

**Evidence:** `or20-gamba-schrader-health | or20-gamba-reynolds-health | or20-gamba-schrader-climate | or20-canning-defazio-health | or20-canning-defazio-corporate-frame`

**Limit:** The official guide identifies the election and who furnished each statement; it does not certify their claims as true. Exact first-publication timing remains qualified, and the April6 preparation certificate is not falsely used as a publication day.

## Direct campaign texts overturn easy assumptions about healthcare divides

In Texas's2020 District25 primary, Heidi Sloan and Julie Oliver both explicitly support Medicare for All. Their donor restrictions differ in stated scope: Sloan rejects corporate and developer money, while Oliver states an all-PAC restriction. In Michigan's2020 District13 primary, Rashida Tlaib and Brenda Jones both explicitly support Medicare for All. Those overlaps come from the candidates' own words rather than interpreting political alliances or donations as policy commitments.

**Evidence:** `tx20-sloan-oliver-mfa | tx20-sloan-oliver-donors | mi20-tlaib-jones-mfa`

**Limit:** The record does not establish identical implementation plans or actual compliance with donor pledges. Defective repeated Sloan questionnaire rows were withheld and replaced with her own pre-primary archived platform.

## Shared commitments include concrete local proposals, not only broad values

The reviewed record includes agreement on completing a transit project, raising taxes through a named amendment, and protecting organizing and bargaining rights. These specific agreements complicate a simple socialist-versus-moderate interpretation.

**Evidence:** `cmp-hb01-green-line | cmp-hb01-millionaire-tax | cmp-hb05-union-organizing | fed24-bowman-latimer-climate`

**Limit:** Agreement on one proposal does not establish identical platforms or eliminate other differences between the candidates.

## What this does not establish

- That every DSA-endorsed candidate supports every DSA organizational policy.
- That other Democrats uniformly oppose single-payer, public housing, union rights or climate action.
- That an omitted topic is opposed, or that a short answer is a complete platform.
- That source counts measure spending, campaign attention, voter salience or electoral effectiveness.
- That repeated national-platform material is independent state-specific campaigning.
- That a publisher's forced-choice survey code captures every qualification in a candidate's answer.
