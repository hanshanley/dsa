    Allentown City Council race: 9 Democrats, 1 Republican vying for 4 seats

    In addition to a competitive mayoral race, four of seven seats on Allentown City Council are up for grabs in this year’s election.

    The legislative body will welcome at least one new face to a four-year term in 2022; President Julio Guridy has decided to run for mayor rather than seek a sixth term on council.

    Three Democratic incumbents face competition from six challengers in the May 18 primary. Republican Tom Houck will join the four Democratic nominees in the general election race.

    Here’s some background on each candidate.

    Tino Babayan

    Babayan announced his candidacy in early January. The Syrian immigrant owns an auto detailing business, has coached soccer in youth organizations for more than 25 years and has been a longtime booster of Allentown School District. He was interviewed for a council seat vacancy in 2016, but wasn’t selected.

    Babayan, 51, said he will pursue affordable housing initiatives, create extracurricular opportunities for youth and support community-oriented policing. Last year, he urged City Council to resist calls to defund the police department.

    Daryl Hendricks

    Hendricks said in December that he’s decided to seek a third term on City Council, where “I feel I can be most effective while steering our city toward a position of fiscal stability.” He briefly considered a mayoral bid.

    Hendricks, 67, is an Allentown native and retired police captain who worked for the city for 38 years. He was first elected to council in 2013 and served as vice president in 2016 and ’17, during which time he often sparred with former Mayor Ed Pawlowski.

    Hendricks served as council president last year, working closely with Mayor Ray O’Connell’s administration on its response to the COVID-19 pandemic. He also clashed with activists and council members Joshua Siegel and Ce-Ce Gerlach over their proposed reforms and funding cuts to the police department. He’s expressed support for implementing the recommendations of a consulting firm that conducted a financial and operational audit of the city in 2019.

    Hendricks also serves on the board of the Allentown Parking Authority and the Allentown Economic Development Corp. He chaired the Healthy Kids Healthy Allentown anti-hunger initiative in 2020, when it experienced a 23% increase in meals served to children.

    Tom Houck

    Houck, 63, is the lone Republican candidate running for City Council this year. He is lifelong Allentown resident and a former city police officer. He retired as a civilian police officer for the Department of Defense in 2018, and also served 33 years in the Air Force Reserve.

    Houck believes Republican representation on City Council is necessary to “bring together and unite the community.” If elected, he will prioritize meeting the needs of lower-income residents and underprivileged youth, and will seek more funding for senior citizens and physically disabled residents. He believes the city needs to be more fiscally responsible, and says he gained experience handling budgets during his police career and military service. Houck is “strongly opposed” to defunding the police department but says the city can do more to address mental health issues and help those experiencing homelessness.

    While he’s never held public office, Houck said he’s active in Lehigh Valley GOP organizations and is involved with the Wounded Warrior Project. He’s a member of several rod and gun clubs and supports several wildlife and nature conservatories, among other nonprofits.

    Cynthia Mota

    Mota, 47, said in December that she will seek a final term on City Council this year. She was appointed in 2012 to fill a vacancy before being elected in 2013 and reelected in 2017. She was tapped as council vice president last week.

    Born in the Dominican Republic, Mota is an Allentown School District graduate with a background in counseling and human services. She runs a small business called CM Centro Multiservices, which provides immigration, notary and translation services. She’s also executive director of Light on the Horizon, a new community nonprofit combating generational poverty.

    Among other legislative efforts, Mota successfully pushed a bill to require window safety guards in rental units with children following the death of a kindergartner in early 2019.

    Santo Napoli

    Napoli, owner of the assembly88 men’s fashion store on Hamilton Street, announced Feb. 9 that he would run for council to serve as a voice for the city’s small-business community.

    Napoli, 48, has owned a number of retail shops downtown since 1999, and is proud that a number of his former employees have gone on to start their own businesses in the city. He chairs the Downtown Allentown Business Alliance and was the architect of the Allentown Merchant Program gift card. He also serves on the Allentown Parking Authority board of directors.

    If elected, Napoli said, he would push the city to do more to encourage entrepreneurship and small-business development, which he says will create job opportunities for residents who too often commute out of the city to find work.

    Napoli, who also manages residential properties, said he would support legislation ensuring landlords provide and maintain safe, code-compliant housing for their tenants. And he said his experience managing budgets has prepared him to address the city’s looming financial challenges.

    Patrick Palmer

    Palmer, a 33-year-old salesman and community organizer, announced his candidacy in February.

    Palmer previously ran for mayor in 2019. He has served on the city’s Human Relations Commission and the Allentown Commercial & Industrial Development Authority board of directors, and is currently vice chairperson of the Allentown Democratic Committee. He advocated for the creation of a city Council of Youth in 2019 following a spate of summer gun violence, during which he was one of the most vocal critics of Mayor Ray O’Connell and former police Chief Tony Alsleben.

    If elected, the Allentown native said he would prioritize fiscal responsibility, noting the considerable structural budget deficits the city faces over the next few years. He said he will also ensure more resources are allocated to addressing mental health-related issues and helping residents experiencing homelessness or housing insecurity. In addition, he’ll advocate for more youth anti-violence programming.

    Justan Parker Fields

    Parker Fields, the founder of Black Lives Matter to Lehigh Valley and executive director of Change Now! Lehigh Valley, announced in November that he would seek a seat on council.

    Last year, Parker Fields organized numerous protests against police brutality and racism following the George Floyd killing in Minneapolis and a forceful arrest outside St. Luke’s Hospital-Sacred Heart in Allentown.

    The 37-year-old customer service manager argued that communities across the Lehigh Valley should no longer have their police forces serve as first responders to mental health crises, domestic disturbances and other similar incidents, and should reallocate portions of police budgets to social workers, mental health advocates and “housing-first initiatives.” He says he’ll bring fresh opinions and fearless courage to council.

    Erik Rodriguez

    Rodriguez, a 25-year-old child care entrepreneur and musician, is running on a “United Youth” slate with candidate Natalie Santos. They believe current elected officials don’t understand the “new challenges” of growing up in the city and are not adequately hearing and considering younger residents’ concerns.

    Rodriguez is an Allentown native and Allen High graduate. He and his wife started a child care center in 2017 that merged with the Miles of Smiles Learning Center in September.

    Rodriguez said he will work to improve city neighborhoods beyond the Hamilton Street corridor transformed in recent years. He said it’s inexcusable that the Center City neighborhood where he grew up has stagnated given its proximity to the considerable investment in downtown’s Neighborhood Improvement Zone. The father of three said he would also prioritize ending homelessness and reimagining public safety, specifically noting that the city must end the overpolicing of youth that leads to a school-to-prison pipeline.

    Natalie Santos

    Santos is a 21-year-old Dieruff High graduate double majoring in psychology and Spanish at Kutztown University, where she also works in student services.

    Santos said that while there have been good changes in Allentown, more significant reform is needed to better serve the city’s Hispanic majority. The Black Lives Matter protests last summer inspired her to get involved and learn more about the social injustices and inequities affecting various city communities. Like Rodriguez, she said she will speak up for the city’s younger residents.

    Their platform also includes supporting residents experiencing homelessness; creating trade and apprenticeship programs and reintroducing a responsible contractor law; and working on legislation that reduces the city’s carbon footprint.

    Ed Zucal

    Zucal said in December that he would seek a second term in office, and formally announced his reelection bid in early March. The 63-year-old worked for the Allentown Police Department for nearly 25 years before retiring in 2016 as a sergeant. He also works as a part-time Lehigh County deputy coroner. After running unsuccessfully for Lehigh County sheriff in 2015, he was elected to council in 2017.

    During his first term, Zucal has on multiple occasions pushed for council to more strictly comply with the city’s home rule charter. He was critical of former police Chief Tony Alsleben’s leadership during the violent summer of 2019, and pushed for his ouster. Last year, Zucal defended the police department when activists and a few of his colleagues voiced criticisms and called for funding cuts.

    In a campaign announcement, Zucal pointed to his attentiveness in scrutinizing the O’Connell administration’s proposed budgets and finances, and detailed his record supporting the city’s police and fire departments. He also noted that he fought to save Allentown State Hospital, which was recently demolished.
