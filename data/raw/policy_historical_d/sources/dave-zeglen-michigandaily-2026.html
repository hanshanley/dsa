Meet the candidates: Dave Zeglen and Aidan Sova face off in Ann Arbor’s Ward 4

by Dominic Apap July 30, 2026July 30, 2026

Share this:

  • Share on X (Opens in new window) X
  • Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook

Leading up to the Aug. 4 Democratic primaries, The Michigan Daily’s Ann Arbor beat interviewed all 12 candidates on the ballot for the Ann Arbor mayoral and City Council races. Aidan Sova requested to submit his responses via email.

Ann Arbor’s 4th Ward represents the southwest part of the city and contains many key landmarks including Michigan Stadium and Briarwood Mall. Progressive Aidan Sova and Democratic socialist Dave Zeglen are running in the Aug. 4 Democratic primary election to replace incumbent Councilmember Dharma Akmon, D-Ward 4.

Sova currently serves as the president of the Ann Arbor District Library Board. In 2022, Sova became the youngest Black person elected in city history at 23 years old. His priorities include providing effective city services, increasing housing supply and affordability and modernizing the city’s public transit. Sova is endorsed by the entire current City Council, mayor Christopher Taylor, Lieutenant Governor Garlin Gilchrist and state Sen. Mallory McMorrow, D-Royal Oak. 

Zeglen, a lecturer at the University of Michigan, has lived in Ann Arbor for four years, and expressed interest in running after discovering the city’s proposed redevelopment of a city-owned parking lot — which he believes should be used to build public housing — to a private developer. Zeglen is running on a platform focused primarily on housing, unions and democracy. His endorsements include mayoral candidate Yousef Rabhi, the Ann Arbor Education Association — the union of Ann Arbor Public School teachers — the Ann Arbor Tenants Union, the Huron Valley Area Labor Federation and U.S. Rep. Rashida Tlaib, D-Mich.

Sova wrote he believes City Council should focus on the simple issues which matter to residents.

“In my conversations with patrons across our community, I kept hearing a consistent call to return to the fundamentals: excellent city services, reliable transportation and connected infrastructure, and diversified housing options,” Sova wrote. “Witnessing these urgent, everyday needs go unaddressed is exactly what motivated me to step up and run for City Council to champion a more accessible Ann Arbor for everyone.”

Zeglen said he is running for City Council in order to empower residents who disagree with the current mayoral administration, and expressed concern with Sova’s ability to push back against Taylor and other Councilmembers. 

“The people of Ann Arbor — when they elect me as a city Councilmember — are electing me to be a dissident voice, not a rubber stamp of the current administration,” Zeglen said. “I very much fear that my opponent will just become a rubber stamp for Taylor and Taylor’s agenda, and I want to give people a real opportunity to have their concerns voiced, because they have been shut out for a very, very long time.”

Sova, however, has a history of opposing the current City Council. After being appointed to the Ann Arbor Human Rights Commission in 2021, while attending the University of Michigan, Sova led a charge to ban the Ann Arbor Police Department from using facial recognition technology. He wrote he believes this past opposition to City Council indicates his commitment to supporting legislation that is popular among residents.

“I want to be entirely clear: I will not be a rubber stamp for any one person or political agenda,” Sova wrote. “As a Human Rights Commissioner, I helped lead a unanimously passed resolution to ban the use of facial recognition software in local policing. Despite the deep popularity of that measure among residents, City Council refused to bring the proposal forward — and that kind of dismissal is simply unacceptable.”

Housing

Zeglen has expressed support for the city’s Comprehensive Land Use Plan, which outlines the city’s goals to build duplexes and triplexes areas previously zoned exclusively for single-family housing. However, he said he believes new housing should be public and advocated for more community engagement in the planning process.

“I don’t think the process was implemented in a fair and equitable way by taking into consideration the concerns of residents in various neighborhoods,” Zeglen said. “I do support the premise of mixed use, as well as having triplex, duplex in single-family residential zones. I think that’s a good thing, but if it’s not public housing, it’s not going to be affordable, simply because of the way the uncapped property tax works in the city.”

Zeglen also criticized the Tax Increment Financing Plan of the Arbor South development, located in Ward 4, which will divert around $350 million in tax revenue to a private developer to reimburse construction costs. The development will include retail, office space and more than 1,000 housing units, including 200 units designated as affordable.

While the two candidates both support increasing affordable housing, Sova wrote public housing alone cannot effectively address affordability concerns, and advocated for more types of housing.

“I am focused on practical, hyper-local solutions that deliver immediate results for our neighbors,” Sova wrote. “My lived experience with housing and food insecurity as the child of a single mother in poverty has taught me that we cannot afford to wait for a single solution to solve a multi-faceted crisis. To truly address our housing crisis, we must pursue a ‘yes-and’ approach — combining public affordable housing with missing-middle housing, transit-oriented development, and community land trusts to build an inclusive Ann Arbor.”

Public Power

Ann Arbor for Public Power is currently collecting signatures to put a proposal on the midterm ballot which would begin the process of municipalizing the city’s electricity. Many members of the current administration, including Mayor Christopher Taylor, oppose the initiative due to the potential costs of buying the grid and engaging in a legal battle with DTE Energy. 

Zeglen supports public power, arguing the project could be financed through revenue bonds. Zeglen said the money used to buy the electrical grid would be paid back using the revenue generated by the grid, citing Lansing’s public utility as an example.

“If we’re paying millions and millions of dollars to DTE, and we’re not getting services or responsiveness back or transparency, we can reinvest that through our revenue bonds, pay it off in 20 to 30 years, and have a revenue-generating utility like Lansing does,” Zeglen said. “Lansing has a public utility and they generate revenue from it — about $20 million a year — that goes back into its social programs.”

Sova wrote he supports the idea of public power, but is focused on the city’s Sustainable Energy Utility, which will allow customers to opt in to sustainable energy as an alternative to DTE.

“City Council should have commissioned a comprehensive, independent study long ago to determine the true acquisition costs and legal realities of a public buyout,” Sova wrote. “We should scale immediate solutions like the SEU while commissioning an independent, data-driven feasibility study to give our community the factual foundation required to pursue full public power responsibly.”

Data centers

Currently, Oracle and OpenAI are jointly constructing a data center to run their upcoming artificial intelligence project, Stargate, in Saline. In Ypsilanti Township, the University and Los Alamos National Laboratory plan to build a data center which will be used, in part, for nuclear weapons research. 

Zeglen criticized the data centers, advocating that Ann Arbor pass a moratorium on their construction and support neighboring communities — including Ypsilanti and Saline — where companies are planning to build them.

“There’s data centers being built in Saline, there’s data centers being built in (Ypsilanti),” Zeglen said. “The ecological destruction that’s going to happen with the Ypsilanti data center is going to affect Ann Arbor. … I think it’s very important that the city get ahead of that — we make sure that we pass a moratorium banning data centers in Ann Arbor, and we form a regional inter-municipal compact with other townships and municipalities to help each other out when data centers do try to roll into different places.”

Sova, who also works as a product lead for Google, did not outright pledge to oppose all data centers. He said while they are unlikely to be built in Ann Arbor due to geographical limitations, any plans must prioritize community needs and input.

“I will refuse to consider any data center proposal that does not operate within a framework prioritizing our community’s resources over corporate convenience,” Sova wrote. “Should any such project proposals arise, engagement with any developer is strictly contingent on a transparent, public process that makes community input the number one priority — listening to residents and ensuring their voices are central to the process from the very beginning.”

If a data center were to be built, Sova advocated for a project labor agreement to protect unionized workers who construct the building and install entirely renewable, closed-loop cooling to protect the city’s water supply and legal assurances that costs won’t be shifted to residents.

AFSCME Contract

The American Federation of State, County & Municipal Employees Local 369, a union of city employees, is in an ongoing contract dispute with the city of Ann Arbor. Its previous contract — which went into effect January 2022 and expired December 2025 — included yearly salary increases during the first three years of the contract in order to meet increased cost of living. However, it included a 0% increase in 2025 due to the city’s concerns over COVID-19’s economic impacts at the time the contract was signed.

In 2023, Sova helped negotiate the AADL contract with the Ann Arbor District Library Associates union, which includes library employees. Sova wrote this experience would be valuable for navigating contract negotiations between unions and the city.

“In navigating complex labor negotiations, I would bring my proven track record of successfully and joyfully collaborating with both of our library system’s unions on comprehensive payment remodeling,” Sova wrote. “My approach on Council will always center on mutual respect and transparent communication, ensuring our hard-working public servants are fairly compensated while maintaining strict fiscal responsibility over our taxpayer dollars.”

Zeglen has attended protests advocating for union workers and laborers. Zeglen said the city needs to better prioritize its workforce, referencing the AFSCME negotiations.

“The city spends a lot of money on outside consultants and private contractors — that is not going back to city workers,” Zeglen said. “(Last year) the mayor gave himself a raise, City Council gave themselves a raise, and the city administrator got a big raise, so I think that’s pretty insulting when there’s 300 AFSCME employees that haven’t gotten a raise.”

Transportation and Pedestrian Safety

Both candidates have focused on strengthening public transportation throughout their campaigns. Sova wants to increase housing density along bus corridors.

Zeglen, who doesn’t own a driver’s license and relies on public transportation, shared that one of his goals is to improve the city’s transit system. He said he wants to make buses free for riders in order to support Washtenaw County’s unhoused population. In FY 2025, bus fares only generated about 5% of TheRide’s total revenue.

“I think the free buses are an equity issue,” Zeglen said. “We have a massive homelessness problem in Washtenaw County … Many of them need to be able to get to warming shelters either in Ypsilanti or in Ann Arbor. I’ve talked to people that work at the warming shelters in Ypsilanti, and they have horror stories about unhoused people having to trek in the middle of the night in freezing weather in February to get between the two cities.”

Sova has advocated for improving walkability in Ward 4 by increasing pedestrian safety and updating infrastructure, and said he hopes to focus on pedestrian infrastructure.

“The Fourth Ward is uniquely defined by its vibrant mix of close-knit residential neighborhoods, major community hallmarks, and transit corridors that connect so many of our residents to the rest of the city,” Sova wrote. “Because of this high-traffic blend, our area uniquely requires an aggressive, targeted focus on pedestrian safety, particularly to fill our persistent sidewalk gaps and repair aging infrastructure.”

The Democratic primary election is on Aug. 4. Ann Arbor residents can vote at any of the city’s 52 polling places from 7 a.m. until 8 p.m.
