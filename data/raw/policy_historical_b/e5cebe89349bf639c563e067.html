The race for Erie City Council: Democrat Allen promises to continue scrutiny if re-elected

Kevin Flowers
Erie Times-News
View Comments

On several occasions, Liz Allen contemplated whether she truly wanted to seek a second four-year term on Erie City Council.

Local governance, Allen has said in previous interviews, can be frustrating. 

But the former Erie Times-News editor and reporter, who was first elected to City Council in 2017, ultimately decided to launch a re-election bid because she feels that city government has plenty of room to grow and improve, and she wants to be a part of that.

"There is much more to do," said Allen, 69, said in a February interview.  "I want to continue to scrutinize the budget and ask the hard questions."

Allen is among eight Democrats and two Republicans in the City Council race.  Four of City Council's seven seats are on the ballot in the May 18 municipal primary.

All seven council seats are currently held by Democrats.

Allen said stability was another reason she decided to seek re-election.  There will almost certainly be at least three new members of City Council in 2022.

Councilman David Brennan declined to enter the race, and Councilman Jim Winarski's third four-year term ends in December. Winarski cannot run again because of city term limit rules.

The fourth seat on the ballot is held by Councilwoman Kathy Schaaf, who announced in December that she would not seek re-election in 2021. 

City Council members serve part-time and are paid $6,000 a year; the council’s president is paid $7,500 annually.  Council members are also eligible for health insurance benefits.

The Erie Times-News asked each of the 10 candidates a series of questions about the City Council race.

Here are Allen's edited responses to those questions. 

What unique ability or skill would you bring to City Council?

I bring the skills I honed as a lifelong journalist to City Council. I research the issues. I press for answers to questions — on proposed ordinances; on budget issues; on actions (or inactions) by previous councils and mayors; on how other municipalities innovate.

I provide context, especially on the length of time some problems have festered in Erie without action. I insist on transparency, not just for council decision-making but also at meetings I attend.

I also value storytelling. To tell another person’s story, you have to listen to their experiences. In journalism, you report that story.

In public service, you figure out how and where to share those stories so that issues can be brought to light and addressed. Last but not least, I’m a watchdog, but now from my council position, instead of the newsroom.

What should city government do right now to control expenses and/or create new revenues?

In previous budget sessions, City Council has suggested specific revenue-enhancers, including increasing the amusement tax from 3% to 5%; requiring rental fees for park pavilions; charging fees for city-produced compost for commercial enterprises; and raising the fee for right-of-way permits for sidewalk dining.

I supported those proposals, although none was implemented, but in the time of COVID-19, these ideas would be counter-productive. The pandemic has decimated our sports and performing arts venues. We need to encourage people to enjoy safe outdoor recreation and we want must support restaurants that offer outdoor dining, rather than creating additional expenses.

We should focus now should be on controlling expenses. City Hall has been shut down for more than a year; many city services have been moved online. What efficiencies has that created? How do we move toward more digital government? Can vacant positions remain unfilled?

Longer-term, we need to follow the recommendations in the PFM report on city finances, especially the report’s advice on how to tackle the costs of employee benefits. Those expenses are not sustainable.

In what specific way would you like to see city’s nearly $80 million in American Rescue Plan funding utilized?

I am educating myself on the ARP by attending webinars sponsored by the National League of Cities and the Pennsylvania Municipal League. Both groups have stressed that we don’t yet know the U.S. Treasury rules for how the funding can be spent. I am pleased that the county executive, the mayor and various other community leaders have started to talk about priorities for the funding, but Erie City Council does not yet have a seat at that table. I have asked that we be included.

In broad terms, the money should be used:

  • To relieve the hardships on small businesses
  • To help tenants and those experiencing homelessness
  • To address inequities in health care
  • To improve child care
  • To expand mental health services
  • To meet the needs of fledgling entrepreneurs
  • To bridge the digital divide with broadband

The National League of Cities gets it right in its FAQ about the ARP: “Make racial equity a part of your plan and ensure that you have set the targets to support those who are often excluded.” When the rules are clear and we set priorities, we should apply that measure to the programs/policies/people receiving funds: Does this decision advance racial equity in Erie? How will that progress be measured?

I also like the way the Brookings Institute frames the issue: “The decisions made in the coming weeks — and over the next year regarding the second tranche of funding — will determine whether cities merely enjoy a brief stimulus or seed a new trajectory of inclusive economic growth.”

It’s not just City Council that needs input on the ARP spending plan. We need to make sure that those who have been marginalized, not just during the pandemic but for decades, get their say. And in the process, I hope we can come up with a better word than “stakeholder,” which allows call to mind someone who already possesses power or prestige. I prefer a simpler term: ordinary people.

A majority of current City Council members have said they favor creating a civilian review board to examine claims of police misconduct. Do you support such a move? Why/why not?

I strongly support the creation of a civilian review board. Previous councils have supported such a board for more than 25 years, yet it always hits a roadblock. The National Association for Civilian Oversight of Law Enforcement can provide the expertise, and we have a local attorney who is familiar with NACOLA and the various models used in other cities.

I deeply admire the hard work being done by the Strengthening Police and Community Partnership group.in Erie. Those members have been having honest, frank conversations and have recommended actions to establish and then grow trust between law enforcement and the minority community. It is not easy to put yourself out there for scrutiny about your attitudes and your actions. It’s even harder to follow up those words with actions, but the city of Erie is

Making progress, for example, in recruiting diverse applicants for police and fire jobs, in reaching young people through the Police Athletic League and in adopting and improving community policing.

Yet police-community relationships in Erie don’t occur in a vacuum. Nationally, we are finally coming to terms with the impact of racism on law enforcement. In Erie, we know our own sad history in that regard – a demeaning video rant by a police officer about a murder victim years ago, a racist email by a now-fired police officer last summer, court settlements made for use of excessive force. We can’t erase those incidents from our collective memories. But we can work together to help our residents know that they have an official avenue where they can take complaints and concerns.

When I worked for the Center for Consumer Affairs at the University of Wisconsin, part of our work was advocating for formal ways for consumers to lodge complaints – about lemon cars, false advertising, exorbitant utility rates, unfair landlord-tenant rules, to name some examples. Consumer-rights advocates championed laws to address those issues, as well as the creation of agencies and arbitration boards to hear consumer complaints. Those actions didn’t destroy the system; rather, they bolstered confidence that transactions could be both legal and fair. We could draw on those lessons for ideas on how to create a workable civilian review board, which would, of course, be responsive to any Erie resident with a complaint.

Have you read the Erie Refocused comprehensive plan? What component(s) of the plan should the city prioritize, and is there any aspect of the plan you disagree with?

I downloaded “Erie Refocused” on my Kindle in 2016, for a book club in which our reading choices focused on social justice and women’s empowerment (“Just Mercy,” “Evicted,” “The Short and Tragic Life of Robert Peace,” “Lean In,” to name a few).

Reading “Erie Refocused” sparked my initial interest in running for Erie City Council. I firmed up my decision to run after attending the 2017bWomen’s March in Washington, D.C., the day after Donald Trump’s inauguration and after a small group of businesspeople invited me to run on an “Erie Refocused” slate and platform.

With its emphasis on city planning, re-investment and neighborhood revitalization, many of the goals in “Erie Refocused” are laudable. But the author’s policy recommendations that some parts of the city aren’t worthy of attention or investment hurt the overall message in “Erie Refocused.” Why would we all join forces for the greater good if some people and places had to be sacrificed to achieve those broad goals?

Fortunately, Mayor Joe Schember’s administration and the dedicated staff, volunteers and organizations working with those who are marginalized recognized this flaw in the report and decided to reject the notion of survival only of the fittest. The mayor’s office, under City Planner Kathy Wyrosdick, produced “Activating Our Vision” in January 2020, with benchmarks and key performance indicators to see how city government can work with its partners to transform Erie. COVID-19 has stalled progress on some goals, and they may have to be adjusted as we adapt to a post-pandemic world.

There are two other areas in which I differ with “Erie Refocused.” The report recommended “an iconic connection between downtown Erie and the Bayfront,” and that language has been interpreted to support PennDOT’s plan to revamp the Bayfront Parkway. At City Council’s public hearing on the PennDOT plan, we heard from 170 people who spoke or offered written testimony about the parkway plans. Two-thirds of the speakers disagreed with PennDOT’s plans, especially for the two roundabouts. Some were well-known urban design activists, but we also heard from small business owners, commuters, east and west bayfront residents and environmentalists and community leaders who objected to the plans, which are, unfortunately, in the process of being implemented due, in part, to “Erie Refocused.”

There are also some odd errors in the report. For example, the report identifies one neighborhood as Trinity Park. Residents there say their neighborhood has never been known as Trinity Park. The city planner has taken note and plans to change the designation. There may be other nomenclatures that have to be fixed.

Overall, it would be an excellent idea for all Erie City Council members, current and upcoming, to reread “Erie Refocused” and share their reactions with each other, five years after the report was issued.

If you had an initiative you want to move forward as a member of City Council, explain how you would successfully create the support needed among fellow council members and/or the mayor’s administration to make it happen.

To move things successfully through council and with the administration, I recommend better use of council committees; drawing on the advice of experts; setting up task forces with community people to tackle large issues; and implementing ongoing training for council members.

Here are some examples:

1. Councilman David Brennan, Councilwoman Kathy Schaaf and I have learned that we need to update/revise our urban agriculture ordinance. We have organized an ad-hoc committee to meet with urban farmers and neighborhood organizations to hear their ideas and learn how other municipalities make it easier for urban ag to thrive.

2. A local resident invited me to learn about the proposed National Infrastructure Bank to pay for the Biden administration’s much-needed infrastructure plan. After attending two webinars organized by the NIB coalition, I asked the Jefferson Educational Society if it could host a digital session on the bank proposal with the national experts. That program took place on April 22 and now council members and the public can view the program, which makes a complex proposal easy to understand and put an Erie-centric perspective on infrastructure needs. This program and other issue-oriented digital offerings, including some with the Pennsylvania Municipal League, have helped me to connect to people and resources that can aid us in enacting local legislation and can make us part of broad-based coalitions for change.

3. To create a civilian review board, I think we should look at the model Erie City Council used in setting up the short-term Historic Preservation Task Force, which led to council’s recent approval of the Historic Review Commission. I would like to see an equity and inclusion task force work on the civilian review board idea, with a deadline, then come back to council with recommendations on how to create a review board. The equity and inclusion task force might also then transition into some type of permanent volunteer commission.

 Councilwoman Kathy Schaaf has successfully advocated for civility training for council members, and her initiative, I hope, can lead to additional training opportunities for council members. I suggested to Kathy that one simple, no-cost training opportunity would be for council members to meet in each other’s neighborhoods and go for a walk together. By walking in the footsteps of other council members, I would gain insights into their lived experiences, and that would assist me in working with them on issues large and small.
