Rabhi challenges Ann Arbor’s Democratic establishment with socialist mayoral bid

By Tom Perkins - February 2, 2026 3:49 pm Updated February 4, 20267:23 pm

State Rep. Yousef Rabhi (D-Ann Arbor) at the University of Michigan on Oct. 3, 2022 | Ken Coleman

Washtenaw County Commissioner and Democratic Socialists of America member Yousef Rabhi will mount a challenge from the left for Ann Arbor mayor against Democratic incumbent Chris Taylor.

The Ann Arbor Democratic primary is Aug. 4, and the primary winner in the liberal stronghold city will likely win in the November general election.  

Rabhi, 37, said he is broadly building his campaign around “bold solutions” to Ann Arbor’s affordability problems, providing community members with a far greater voice in solving city issues, and taking meaningful steps to insulate the city from Trump administration abuses, especially on immigration.  

Among other proposals, he is calling for a deep investment in public ownership and stronger city services with the aim of reducing costs for working-class residents. Rabhi said he would achieve that in part by ending the city’s reliance on out-of-town consultants, limiting tax incentives for developers, reversing the privatization of city services, and making “historic investments” in affordable, publicly owned housing and co-ops. 

Rabhi, a former state representative, is also a strong supporter of municipalizing the city’s energy grid as a measure to lower prices and improve reliability. 

Though Ann Arbor is a liberal stronghold, Rabhi would be among the only candidates in recent years to offer a vision from the Bernie Sanders wing of the Democratic Party. The Ann Arbor City Council has long been dominated by two warring Democratic factions who generally offer a similar moderate political philosophy. Rabhi said he would “offer a third vision.” 

“I’m not part of any faction—I’m part of a faction of the people,” Rabhi said. “I believe in the principles of working together as a community, and finding grassroots solutions to problems.”

The broader vision is a government that makes life easier for residents, and a restored sense of civic pride, Rabhi said. 

He was born and raised in Ann Arbor and served as a state representative in the area from  2017-2023, until he was termed out. He was the House Democratic Floor Leader, the number two position in the caucus, during his final two sessions, and earned a reputation as the chambers’ leftmost representative. He described himself as a sharp critic of DTE who fought against money in politics, for labor rights, for sustainability, and for single-payer health care. 

Rabhi was elected to the Washtenaw County Board of Commissioners in 2023, and previously served on the board for six years beginning at 22 years old, in 2011. He currently serves as legislative director for the Michigan Nurses Association union.  

Is Ann Arbor ready for a leftist, outsider mayor? Rabhi’s campaign comes on the heels of DSA member Zohran Mamdani’s upset win in New York City, and amid a growing frustration nationally with many establishment Democrats’ tepid responses to the Trump administration and affordability issues. 

Ending privatization and cutting consultants to reinvest in city services

A Rabhi administration would represent a break from Ann Arbor’s machine politics, said Lauren Gaboury, co-chair of the Huron Valley chapter of the Democratic Socialists of America, which endorsed Rabhi. 

“The privatization and corporatization of Ann Arbor is not helping the people who make Ann Arbor run,” Gaboury said. “The people who live in Ann Arbor and can’t afford to live in it anymore are not doing well, and we need someone like Yousef on the inside who is going to make changes that help the working class.” 

Rabhi took aim at the consultant class that he said guides many of city council’s decision-making processes. Rabhi said Ann Arbor “has over 100,000 consultants – our residents.”

“There’s a clear feeling that people are not being heard by politicians and the public is not at the center of the decision-making process,” he said. The city’s current public engagement efforts are often “performative,” and there should be “more grassroots input,” he added. 

The money saved on consultant expenses could be re-invested to strengthen city services that reduce residents’ costs, Rabhi said. He claimed the city eliminated a free curbside leaf collection program on his street during fall, but it scrapped the program, adding that soon after, he received a flyer from a company offering to do it for the season for $75. 

While the city did end its bulk leaf pickup program in 2010, it switched instead to a curbside pickup program which picks up leaves and yard debris placed in composting bags or carts.

He also questioned the city’s decision to spend $500,000 on private supplemental snow plowing and privatizing downtown garbage collection. He said he would invest that money in “boots on the ground” city employees and city-run programs like clearing snow from sidewalks, repairing potholes, or increasing bathroom facilities downtown. 

“Any time you privatize a service, you lose control, you lose quality, you lose workers’ rights because it’s not union, but one of the biggest things you lose is civic pride,” Rabhi said. He also called for more city support for small businesses downtown, either by lowering parking rates, holding more festivals, or other measures. 

Rabhi also questioned the city’s decision to hire a former DTE Energy executive to establish a Sustainable Energy Utility (SEU). He has advocated for eliminating DTE Energy from the equation altogether by establishing a public power company in Ann Arbor. 

The SEU backed by city council would leave the grid in DTE’s control. That is a problem because DTE’s failure to maintain the grid is behind the reliability issues, Rabhi said. Meanwhile, DTE’s profits, lobbying, campaign contributions, executives’ salaries and other expenses that come with a private company are a main driver of its high rates, he added. 

Rabhi said he supports some brownfield tax incentives that help remediate contaminated sites because Michigan lacks laws that force polluters to pay for their cleanups. But he noted that developers often receive “excessive” tax breaks. 

He said he supports a mixed-use project at 841 Broadway Street, but added that it received “excessive” brownfield incentives beyond what was needed for the site’s cleanup. He also did not support an additional $20 million public subsidy the state granted the project’s developers to build a “public” park that would be privately owned.

“We need to draw a hard line at spending public resources to support private profits,” Rabhi said.  

On immigration, he said local law enforcement should be deployed to protect people from ICE, and he spearheaded the county-level creation of ICE-free zones that prohibits the agency from warrantless searches on county property. The Washtenaw County Sheriff’s Office has pledged to enforce the measure, Rabhi said.
